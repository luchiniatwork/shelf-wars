# Research Digest — THEME, NARRATIVE & IMMERSION in Board Games

Research note: mainstream search engines (Google/Bing/DDG/Mojeek/searx) rate-limited or bot-blocked this research environment. Searches were therefore executed against site-internal search APIs (WordPress REST) of the canonical designer/publisher blogs (Stonemaier Games, Board Game Design Lab, League of Gamemakers, PrintNinja, Meeple Like Us), the Wikipedia API, and OpenLibrary. 25+ queries were run; 15 full pages fetched and extracted. Community forums (BGG, Reddit) were IP-blocked; community-consensus claims below are flagged where they could not be independently verified this session.

## Core frameworks & models

- **Ludonarrative dissonance — Clint Hocking (2007).** Coined in an October 2007 blog post about *BioShock*: the conflict between the narrative told through non-interactive elements (cutscenes, scripted story) and the narrative told through gameplay. Hocking argued *BioShock* promotes self-interest through gameplay but selflessness through story, violating "aesthetic distance." Frédéric Seraphine (Univ. of Tokyo, 2016 literature review) formalized the cause as opposition between **incentives** and **directives** within the ludic structure vs. the narrative structure. Term imported from video games; tabletop designers (e.g., Geoff Engelstein in *GameTek*) apply it to theme-mechanism mismatch.
- **MDA framework (Mechanics–Dynamics–Aesthetics) — Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek (2004).** Mechanics = rules/basic actions; Dynamics = run-time behavior of mechanics acting on player input; Aesthetics = emotional responses evoked. Eight aesthetics: **sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission**. Designers build M→D→A; players experience A→D→M. Theme lives primarily in "fantasy" and "narrative" aesthetics. (Wikipedia notes the article needs more citations — treat as industry-standard but academically thin.)
- **Three starting points doctrine — Gabe Barrett / Board Game Design Lab ("How to Design a Board Game").** "Some designers start with theme; others start with a core mechanism; and more recently, some designers start with a specific experience in mind. Honestly, there's no wrong answer, and all three methods have led to games considered to be the 'best of all time.'" The experience-first variant is the de facto hybrid "hook" approach: define target audience + desired player feelings ("heroic, thoughtful, paranoid"), then pick theme and mechanisms to serve them.
- **Verisimilitude (VST) — Anthony Rando & Eric Cesare (League of Gamemakers, 2014).** Giving design choices "the attribute of appearing real or truthful" so players suspend disbelief. Explicitly distinguished from realism ("real life is boring"). Equivalent to *Magic: The Gathering*'s "top-down design" / card flavor. Anchoring fictional elements to real-world reference points (size, temperament, encumbrance) makes them memorable and believable.
- **Toybox appeal — Dan Letzring (League of Gamemakers, mash-up themes article).** The appeal of dumping genre toys together (Smash Up, Legendary); deck-builder shuffling mirrors the toybox. Mash-up themes succeed when each faction's asymmetric mechanisms "reflect the essence" of its trope.
- **Theme-as-mapping / thematic integration — Sarah Shipp.** Her book *Thematic Integration in Board Game Design* (CRC Press, 2024 — verified via OpenLibrary) is the only full academic-style monograph on the subject; argues for evaluating how mechanisms instantiate theme rather than whether a game "has" theme.
- **Campaign game definition — Jamey Stegmaier (2021).** "A game designed specifically to be played over multiple sessions with some connecting narrative and persistent elements from game to game," with **micro endings** (pause points, episode cleanup/setup) and **macro endings** (overarching goal).

## Catalog / techniques

**Theme-first vs mechanism-first vs experience-first (with documented examples):**
- Theme-first: Jeff Cornelius's *Threads* — designed "for knitters"; designer learned knitting terminology first, then mapped mechanisms onto it ("you can tell the designer is a knitter" — Reddit commenter; he isn't).
- Mechanism-first reworked thematically: *Expedite* (Cosmic Wombat) — win condition changed from 100 VP to $100M because abstract VP broke the CEO fantasy; *Campaign Trail* — candidate cards redesigned backstory-first, then mechanisms derived from character traits.
- Publisher gate: Stonemaier (~3,000 submissions over 9 years) asks, per game: does it bring joy worldwide, do we love it, does it fit our "12 tenets" vibe, do we already publish a similar game *mechanically or thematically*, evergreen potential, are we excited to teach it repeatedly. Theme redundancy alone is grounds for rejection (a winemaking game would be rejected because Viticulture exists).

**Ludonarrative coherence — paired exemplars:**
- GOOD: *Wingspan* — Stegmaier signed it because Hargrave had designed ~100 unique bird cards "where the theme directly inspired the mechanisms" (he calls this hard: "just how hard it is to design a few dozen unique cards where the theme directly inspired the mechanisms").
- GOOD: *Spirit Island* (R. Eric Reuss, 2017) — deliberate inversion of colonizer games; invader pieces made white specifically to break light=good coding (Wikipedia, citing Ars Technica). Called by The Atlantic (Luke Winkie, 2021, "The Board Games That Ask You to Reenact Colonialism") "one of the few flat-out anti-colonial games."
- GOOD: *Smash Up/Legendary* — shuffle-mixing mechanisms mirror the toybox fantasy.
- BAD/dissonant (community-documented, verify before citing specifics): colonial-theme Euros criticized in The Atlantic piece; historical slave-trade adjacency in older games. *Video game* canonical bad pairs (Hocking's BioShock, Uncharted's "likable rogue who slaughters hundreds") transfer as cautionary analogs.
- Nuance: theme independence is survivable — abstract/pasted-on themes still sell if weight and theme match (League naming article: pun titles only acceptable for light, <30 min games).

**Worldbuilding techniques (tabletop-specific):**
- Mechanisms as lore: simultaneous action selection simulating a food fight's blink-of-an-eye chaos (*What the Food?!*).
- Balance as lore: sanity-loss ratio scaled to offense severity (*Bad Roommate*: sweater vs. car); encumbrance reducing movement.
- Art/graphic design as lore delivery: player boards shaped like desks in *Hansa Teutonica*; tunnel-digging board art in *Euphoria*.
- Flavor discipline: lore embedded in components and card titles rather than wall-of-text (VST article's core advice: leave time-scale abstractions unexplained when explanation adds nothing).
- Backstory-first character design: enumerate all archetypes/backstories/personality traits, then derive mechanisms (Campaign Trail method).

**Immersion levers:**
- Currency/victory condition rename to in-world terms (VP → $100M).
- Components, flavor text, art, story as the four named immersion channels (Cornelius).
- Production values as immersion (*Fog of Love* praised as "remarkable package... physical thing on your table" — Meeple Like Us).
- Player-count truth: campaign games are played 2–4p by 91% of players; only 6% primarily solo (Stonemaier Ambassador survey, 2021; reader survey: 20% solo) — but Stegmaier still calls solo "a necessity."

## Numbers, heuristics & rules of thumb

- **Naming length:** 1–2 words ideal; "every word you use above a single word is minus points" (Peter Vaughan, League). Pun titles ⇒ game must be <30 min and light (Mike Domeny).
- **Campaign engagement:** players average ~2 campaign games/year played; ~3 sitting unplayed; estimated non-campaign:campaign play ratio ~30:1; ~70% have started a campaign they never finished; 30% of those feel regret/disappointment; only 21–29% have completed every campaign started. All from Stegmaier's 2021 surveys; ambassador vs. public-reader numbers differ notably (solo 6% vs 20%; all-completed 21% vs 29%).
- **Representation baseline:** among the top 200 BGG games as of 2018, **94% of designers were white men** (Elizabeth Hargrave). Nick Yee's board-gamer motivation survey (April 2017): n>90,000; 72.7% male, 26.0% female, 1.1% other.
- **Economics:** Stonemaier spent **$150,000 total on Wingspan** covering art + graphic design + manufacturing + freight before revenue (Jan 2019 first English sale). Advances are **not standard industry practice**; Stonemaier instituted a standard **$10,000 designer advance** (kept even if unpublished/abandoned) in Aug 2021. Royalties typically paid quarterly by most publishers; Stonemaier pays monthly on revenue received (incl. localization partners).
- **Art commissioning process (Stegmaier):** gauge interest/availability → request 1–2 prepaid sample illustrations → share cost ranges per illustration type with total counts ("several months' worth of work" is itself leverage) → short plain-language contract; publisher owns product-specific illustrations (creator ≠ owner); artist gets self-promotion/prints rights only after official announcement (anti-spoiler timing).
- **Marketing dependency:** excitement for a game "is largely dependent on the game itself (theme, mechanisms, etc), not the inclusion of a campaign" (Stegmaier survey conclusion) — i.e., theme+hook sells; campaign structure alone doesn't. The widely repeated industry adage "theme gets them to buy it, mechanisms get them to replay/review it" is **[community adage — no single authoritative origin verified this session]**.
- **[contested]** MDA's eight aesthetics are widely cited in industry education but Wikipedia flags the article as under-cited.
- **[contested]** GMT's *Scramble for Africa* 2019 cancellation and Days of Wonder's 2015 *Five Tribes* slave→fakir card replacement are frequently cited sensitivity-case-studies; not re-verified this session (news sites reachable but un-fetched).

## Best-practice checklists

**Naming a game (League of Gamemakers, "Ask the League" + Game Name Gauntlet):**
1. Convey feel/spirit/setting; ideally telegraph weight and style too.
2. 1–2 words; easy to remember, say, and spell.
3. Match title tone to weight: complex/uncommon words signal heavy games (Tzolk'in, Twilight Imperium); puns only for light <30-min games.
4. Be unambiguous — counterexample: "Summoner Wars" vs. "Summoners War."
5. Be original — check BGG database, app stores, and existing products for collisions.
6. Check trademark-ability early (USPTO; games fall in Nice Class 28); consider checking domain availability.
7. Crowdsource candidates (social networks, playtesters); provisionally title early so playtesters can reference it; get objective feedback — what resonates with you may not with others.

**Theme-mechanism resonance check (synthesized from VST + Cornelius + Stegmaier):**
1. Can each core mechanism be explained in one thematic sentence? (food fight simultaneity test)
2. Do win conditions/resources use in-world nouns (money, influence), not abstract VP where avoidable?
3. Do relative costs/effects match in-world severity/scale (sweater-vs-car ratio test)?
4. Do factions/characters' mechanisms reflect their trope's essence (Zerpang asymmetry test)?
5. Could you reskin it? If yes trivially (Love Letter-style), theme is decorative — acceptable for light games, fatal for immersive/campaign pitches.
6. For theme-first: immerse in the domain first (terminology, practice) before mechanism mapping.

**Cultural sensitivity process (Stegmaier + Isaac Childress):**
1. Assume "you don't know what you don't know" — fictional worlds are not exempt ("nothing is created in a vacuum... our biases have a tendency to cause harm anyway" — Childress).
2. Hire a cultural consultant at final-files stage (Stonemaier did for Tapestry expansion #2; Childress for Frosthaven). Named practitioners in fetched sources: **James Mendez Hodes** (Frosthaven), **Kate Edwards** (hired for *Fled*).
3. Brief: internal consistency of depicted cultures; no harmful co-option of real-world terms/ideas; collaborative fixes, not just problem-flagging — "makes the game more ethical... and also simply just makes it better" (Childress).
4. Frame goal as "less harm and more joy"; proactive beats reactive.

**Representation in theme/art (Hargrave + Bezdenejnih-Snyder guest posts):**
1. Believe reported harms; absence of witnessed incidents ≠ absence.
2. Don't attribute success to gendered theme assumptions ("women like pretty/bird/low-conflict games" — each debunked by surveyed women designers); individual preference > demographic generalization.
3. Use motivation-survey data (Yee) to broaden theme/mechanism coverage, not to stereotype.
4. Diversify who designs/develops — pipeline skew (94% white men, top-200 BGG 2018) is structural, not necessarily overt discrimination.

**Art commissioning (Stonemaier model):**
1. Scope estimate up front; 1–2 prepaid samples before commitment.
2. Cost ranges stated per illustration type; offer volume (months of work) as negotiating value.
3. Short plain-language contract: publisher owns work-for-hire product art; artist retains self-promotion/print rights post-announcement.
4. Respect artists who decline ownership terms; missing ownership clauses "caused quite a bit of frustration."

## Common pitfalls & failure modes

- **Pasted-on theme / theme as wallpaper:** mechanisms untouched by theme; breaks when players ask "why am I doing this in-world?" (Expedite's VP problem). Survivable only in light/abstract-positioned games.
- **Ludonarrative dissonance (tabletop form):** incentives reward behavior the fiction condemns (Hocking's BioShock pattern); Seraphine's incentive-vs-directive split is the diagnostic.
- **Realism instead of verisimilitude:** simulating reality's tedium (bathroom breaks, wool regrowth) rather than anchoring believability. VST ≠ realism.
- **Theme-mechanism mismatch in tone/weight:** heavy mechanisms under a pun title; light chaos under an epic name (League naming consensus).
- **Reskin breakage:** mechanisms that reference theme-specific nouns/characters die under retheme; theme-independent cores (Love Letter; the BSG-Express→*Dark Moon* lineage is the community-cited survival case — **[verify before citing]**) reskin cleanly. Asymmetric faction mechanisms bound to trope essence (Zerpang) resist reskin by design.
- **Wall-of-text lore:** worldbuilding dumped in rulebook passages instead of distributed across card titles, flavor, component shape, and board geography (VST/Hansa Teutonica lesson).
- **Colonial/appropriation blind spots:** colonizer-fantasy defaults (The Atlantic's "reenact colonialism" critique); "it's fantasy so it's harmless" fallacy (Childress). Fix: cultural consultants, inverted premises (Spirit Island).
- **Demographic stereotyping in theme choices:** "female-friendly theme" reasoning; publishers pitching by gender assumption (Bezdenejnih-Snyder).
- **Campaign fatigue as market risk:** backlog (~3 unplayed), unfinished campaigns (~70%), regret (30%) ⇒ buyers become selective; campaign structure is not itself a selling point.
- **Marketing/review split risk:** a hot theme/IP drives first sales but mechanisms drive longevity, reviews, and evergreen status; publishers explicitly screen for both (Stonemaier similar-game and evergreen questions).
- **IP licensing seduction:** licensed IPs impose approvals, royalties, and bureaucracy; BGDL's licensed-IP episodes (Daryl Andrews; Nolan Nasser) and reader consensus: fine for experienced designers, a trap for first-timers — original IP builds your own brand/equity and avoids licensor interference.

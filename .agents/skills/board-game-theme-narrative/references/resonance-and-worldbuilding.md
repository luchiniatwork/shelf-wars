# Resonance & Worldbuilding — Deep Reference

Load when auditing theme-mechanism fit, writing lore/flavor, designing campaign/legacy narrative, assessing
reskin risk, or reviewing cultural-sensitivity case studies. Everything here expands SKILL.md sections B–D,
G–H.

## 1. Ludonarrative coherence — theory

- **Ludonarrative dissonance** — coined by Clint Hocking in an October 2007 blog post about *BioShock*: the
  conflict between the narrative told through non-interactive elements and the narrative told through
  gameplay. *BioShock* promotes self-interest through play but selflessness through story — a "violation of
  aesthetic distance." (Verified via Wikipedia; term imported from video games, now standard in tabletop
  criticism, e.g., Geoff Engelstein's *GameTek*.)
- **Formal diagnostic** — Frédéric Seraphine (Univ. of Tokyo, 2016 literature review): dissonance = an
  opposition between **incentives** and **directives** across the ludic structure (rules/rewards) and the
  narrative structure (story/fiction). Tabletop translation:
  - *Incentive* = what scoring/optimality rewards.
  - *Directive* = what the fiction says you are/should do.
  - Dissonance when incentive ⇒ behavior the directive condemns.
- **MDA placement** (Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek, 2004): theme lives chiefly in the **fantasy** and
  **narrative** aesthetics. Designers build Mechanics→Dynamics→Aesthetics; players experience the reverse —
  so theme is the *first thing players touch* and the last thing designers control. (Industry-standard but
  academically thin [contested citations]; see `board-game-design-theory`.)
- **Thematic integration** — Sarah Shipp, *Thematic Integration in Board Game Design* (CRC Press, 2024), the
  only full monograph on the subject: evaluate *how* mechanisms instantiate theme rather than whether a game
  "has" theme. Useful rubric: for each mechanism, name the story beat it enacts.

## 2. Paired exemplars — good vs. bad

### Coherent (cite these as positive patterns)
| Game | Resonance mechanism |
|------|--------------------|
| **Wingspan** (Hargrave, 2019) | 170 unique bird cards; powers derived from real bird behavior (nest type, wingspan, diet). Stegmaier signed it because "the theme directly inspired the mechanisms," which he notes is hard to do across dozens of unique cards. |
| **Spirit Island** (Reuss, 2017) | Deliberate inversion of colonizer games: players are the island's spirits repelling invaders. Invader pieces are white specifically to break light=good/dark=bad coding (Wikipedia, citing Draper, New York Times, Aug 2019); Ars Technica reviewed it as an anti-colonialist design. The Atlantic (2021): "one of the few flat-out anti-colonial games." |
| **Smash Up** / **Legendary** | Shuffle-two-factions deckbuilding *is* the toybox fantasy: dumping genre toys together. Mechanism enacts premise (Dan Letzring's "toybox appeal"). |
| **What the Food?!** | Simultaneous action selection = a food fight's blink-of-an-eye chaos. Mechanism as simulation of fiction. |
| **Fog of Love** | Praised as a "remarkable package... physical thing on your table" (Meeple Like Us); production values carry the romantic-fiction premise. |
| **Freedom: The Underground Railroad** | Co-op structure makes players fight the slave system rather than play it; widely cited as respectful historical treatment via structure, not just theme paint. |

### Dissonant / fragile (diagnostic patterns)
| Pattern | Example / source | Failure |
|---------|------------------|---------|
| Abstract VP under an immersive premise | *Expedite* (pre-fix): win at 100 VP broke the CEO fantasy → changed to $100M [contested — source not located; community-cited case] | Win condition named outside the fiction |
| Colonial-fantasy optimization | Euro games where players efficiently extract/settle; Luke Winkie, "The Board Games That Ask You to Reenact Colonialism" (The Atlantic, 2021) | Incentive (optimize extraction) contradicts modern reader's directive; ages badly, draws press criticism |
| Slave-trade adjacency in older titles | *Five Tribes* "slave" cards → replaced with "fakirs" by Days of Wonder (2015) [contested — not re-verified in-session] | Fiction nouns carry real-world harm; retrofits are costly |
| Genocide-as-map-paint | GMT's *Scramble for Africa* pulled from P500 in 2019 after community outcry [contested — not re-verified in-session] | Publisher-stage kill; theme choice killed an otherwise funded product |
| Video-game canonical cases | Hocking's *BioShock*; *Uncharted*'s "likable rogue who slaughters hundreds" (Matulef; the studio named a trophy "Ludonarrative Dissonance") | Use as analogy when explaining the concept to designers |

**Nuance:** theme independence is survivable when weight and theme match. Abstract/pasted-on themes sell in
light games (League naming consensus: pun titles acceptable only under ~30 min). The failure mode is
*promising* immersion and *delivering* wallpaper.

## 3. Verisimilitude (VST) — Rando & Cesare, League of Gamemakers (2014)

Definition: giving design choices "the attribute of appearing real or truthful" so players suspend
disbelief. Equivalent to M:tG's "card flavor" / top-down design.

Rules from the source article:
1. **VST ≠ realism.** "Real life is boring" — no bathroom-break dice rolls. Escapism is the point.
2. **Anchor the fictional in the real.** Reptilian aliens that are human-sized, cold-blooded, quick to anger
   = believable (anchored to known science). Chicken-sized with the strength of fifty men = players forget or
   reject it. Give one or two real-world reference points per fictional element.
3. **Catan as the canonical VST engine:** fictional land, real colonial logic (roads, settlements, terrain
   resources). Dice-as-time-elapsed is a benign abstraction — unexplained, and better for it.
4. **Leave benign abstractions unexplained.** Explain only what players actually stumble over; explanation
   of everything reads as apology.

## 4. Worldbuilding channels (distribute lore across all of them)

| Channel | Technique | Exemplar |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| Card/component titles | In-world nouns everywhere; mechanical subtitle second or never | Wingspan bird names + Latin |
| Flavor text | One line, consistent voice, only where it adds character; never on surfaces read repeatedly in play | — |
| Mechanisms as lore | Mechanism simulates the story beat (food-fight simultaneity) | What the Food?! |
| Balance as lore | Magnitudes scaled to in-world severity (sweater vs. car sanity loss) | Bad Roommate (Rando & Cesare) |
| Board geography | The map tells the world's story: where things are is lore | Euphoria's tunnel art showing the digging you do |
| Component shape | Physical form echoes fiction | Hansa Teutonica player boards shaped like desks |
| Art | Style/mood as world exposition | Euphoria, Scythe (Rozalski's 1920+ world) |
| In-world currency names | VP → money/influence/sanity | Expedite's $100M [contested — see §2] |

**Flavor-text discipline:** cut any line that (a) repeats what the title/art says, (b) explains a benign
abstraction (VST rule 4), or (c) sits on a reference surface players re-read (player aids, frequently used
action spaces). Lore density belongs on unique, low-frequency components (event cards, legacy stickers).

**Backstory-first character design (Campaign Trail method):** enumerate all archetypes, backstories, and
personality traits first; then derive each character's mechanisms from those traits. Reverses the usual
mechanism-then-paint order and produces trope-true asymmetry (SKILL.md principle 6).

**Theme-first immersion protocol (Jeff Cornelius, Threads):** learn the domain's terminology and practice
before mechanism mapping; goal is insiders saying "the designer gets it." Four immersion channels per
Cornelius: components, flavor text, art, story.

## 5. Narrative design — arcs, campaign & legacy

- **Campaign game definition** (Stegmaier, 2021): "designed specifically to be played over multiple sessions
  with some connecting narrative and persistent elements from game to game."
- **Required structures:**
  - **Micro endings** — pause points with episode cleanup/setup, so a group can stop after any session.
  - **Macro ending** — one overarching goal that closes the campaign.
- **Beat budgeting:** players average ~2 campaign games/year and own ~3 unplayed; ~70% have started a
  campaign they never finished; only 21–29% completed every campaign they started; 30% of abandoners feel
  regret (Stegmaier 2021 surveys). Implication: front-load value — don't bury your best content at episode 9
  of 10; make early sessions complete-feeling.
- **Audience sizing:** 91% of campaign play is 2–4 players; solo-primary is 6% (ambassador survey) to 20%
  (reader survey), but include solo anyway — Stegmaier calls it "a necessity" (delegate the automa design to
  `board-game-solo-coop-design`).
- **Emergent vs. scripted story:** emergent stories (mechanism-generated anecdotes players retell) are the
  medium's native strength; scripted arcs need a campaign/legacy container to survive shuffled session
  order. Prefer mechanisms that *produce* anecdotes (extreme outcomes, named characters that can die,
  persistent scars) over paragraphs of plotted fiction.
- **Marketing truth:** "excitement for a game is largely dependent on the game itself (theme, mechanisms,
  etc.), not the inclusion of a campaign" (Stegmaier survey conclusion). A campaign badge is not a hook.

## 6. Reskin risk — case studies

| Case | Outcome | Lesson |
|------|---------|--------|
| **Love Letter** (Kanai) | 16-card core reskinned into dozens of editions (licensed and original) | Theme-independent core = maximal reskin surface; deliberate architecture choice |
| **BSG Express → Dark Moon** | Community-cited survival case: mechanism core carried a new fiction [contested lineage detail] | Dice-driven social-deduction cores travel well |
| **Zerpang-style trope-bound asymmetry** | Faction mechanisms bound to trope essence resist reskin | Feature for immersion; bug if publisher wants a different license — decide deliberately |
| **Theme-noun entanglement** | Card text referencing named places/characters | Any retheme forces text rewrite; keep mechanism text theme-clean where reskin is plausible |

**Pre-pitch reskin check:** if the publisher's likely retheme (licensed IP, house setting) would require
rewriting mechanism text, say so in the pitch and price the rework.

## 7. Cultural sensitivity — process and case studies

**Process (Stonemaier / Childres model):**
1. "You don't know what you don't know" (Stegmaier). Fictional worlds are not exempt: "nothing is created in
   a vacuum... our biases have a tendency to cause harm anyway" (Isaac Childres).
2. Hire a cultural consultant at final-files stage. Verified engagements: Stonemaier for Tapestry
   expansion #2; James Mendez Hodes for **Frosthaven**; Kate Edwards for **Fled** (hired mid-campaign to fix
   tone concerns raised by backers).
3. Brief (Childres/Hodes scope): internal consistency of depicted cultures; no harmful co-option of
   real-world terms/ideas; **collaborative fixes, not just problem-flagging** — "not only makes the game
   more ethical and welcoming to a wider audience, but also simply just makes it better."
4. Frame the goal as "less harm and more joy" (Childres). Proactive beats reactive: a consultant at final
   files costs a fraction of a cancelled print run or a public apology.
5. Also see the Ludology podcast episode on cultural consulting (referenced by Stegmaier).

**Case studies:** Five Tribes fakir replacement (2015) [contested]; GMT Scramble for Africa cancellation
(2019) [contested]; Spirit Island as the positive inversion; Freedom: The Underground Railroad as respectful
historical structure.

## 8. Representation & historical accuracy

- **Baseline skew:** 94% of top-200 BGG game designers were white men as of 2018 (Elizabeth Hargrave).
  Nick Yee's motivation survey (Quantic Foundry, Apr 2017, n>90,000): 72.7% male, 26.0% female, 1.1% other.
- **Process rules (Hargrave; Bezdenejnih-Snyder guest posts):**
  1. Believe reported harms; absence of witnessed incidents ≠ absence of harm.
  2. Never attribute design success to gendered theme assumptions ("women like pretty/bird/low-conflict
     games" — each debunked by surveyed women designers). Individual preference ≫ demographic generalization.
  3. Use motivation-survey data to *broaden* theme/mechanism coverage, not to stereotype.
  4. Diversify who designs/develops; the skew is structural, not necessarily overt discrimination.
- **Historical-accuracy debates:** the Atlantic critique (2021) shows critics now evaluate the *values* a
  historical game optimizes, not just its set dressing. Practical rule: pick one of three stances —
  simulation (model the harms honestly, e.g., wargame tradition), respectful treatment (structure sides with
  the harmed, e.g., Freedom), or declared entertainment framing (pulp, openly ahistorical) — and disclose the
  stance in designer notes. Marketing "accuracy" while sanitizing harms is the worst of both.
